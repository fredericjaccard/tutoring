Who I work with

I specialise in helping students who follow international curricula such as the IB MYP, IB DP Mathematics AI SL, and IGCSE. I also support students following other mathematics curricula.

I offer enrichment programmes in mathematics and computer science for curious students who need more challenge than they find in the regular classroom. I encourage them to explore surprising ideas and discover the magic of mathematics.

How I teach

I build a relationship in which students feel comfortable asking questions, showing me where they get stuck and testing their ideas. I listen carefully and adapt the lesson as their understanding develops.

I want students to understand why a method works and explain it in their own words. I give them time to explore, offer a hint when they need one and use technology to support that work. As they see what they can figure out for themselves, they gain confidence in solving unfamiliar problems.

How online lessons work

We meet on Zoom and work through problems together on a shared interactive whiteboard. I can help with homework and test preparation, including building a web application so your child can practise independently between lessons.

After each lesson, I send you a short summary of what we covered and direct links to any web applications I created for your child.

For young mathematicians who enjoy challenging problems and playing with ideas. We explore puzzles, elegant proofs and strategy games. IB MYP · Criterion B · Investigating patterns

How many tiles in the next figure?

Explore the growing T-shapes. Record how many tiles each figure uses and predict how many you would need for Figure 20.

Figure 1

Explain and justify your rule

I would ask you to write a rule for Figure n and check it against two figures. Then point to the tiles as you explain why your rule works for any figure.

See one justification

I would start with the centre tile. Can you see n tiles to its left, n to its right and n below it? This gives 3n + 1. Show me how you would get the same rule by counting the top row first.

Can any figure in this pattern have exactly 944 tiles? Use your rule to justify your answer.

Check your reasoning

I would ask you to set aside the centre tile first. Could you share the remaining 943 tiles equally across three arms? Since 943 is not divisible by 3, no figure can have exactly 944 tiles.

MYP · Basketball and quadraticsWill the shot go in?

MYP 5 · Quadratic modelling

Will the shot go in?

The ball follows h(x) = 4 − 0.15(x − 3)², where x is the horizontal distance from the player and h(x) is the height, both in metres. Where should you place the 3.05 m hoop to catch the ball on its way down? Move the hoop, then take the shot to test your answer.

Explore the mathematics

I would show you three ways to write the same curve. As we compare them, look for what each form reveals about the curve. Here, x is the horizontal distance and h(x) is the height, both in metres.

  • Vertex form: h(x) = 4 − 0.15(x − 3)². The peak is 4 m at x = 3.
  • Expanded form: h(x) = −0.15x² + 0.90x + 2.65. The ball starts at 2.65 m when x = 0.
  • Factored form: h(x) = −0.15(x − 3 + √(80/3))(x − 3 − √(80/3)). The roots give the ground-level crossings of the model.

I would ask you to set the vertex form equal to the rim height of 3.05 m. You will get x = 3 ± √(19/3), or about 0.48 m and 5.52 m. Look at those two points on the curve. Which one catches the ball on its way down? Try putting the hoop 5.5 m from the player and take the shot.

IB DP AI SL · Voronoi diagramsWhich station is closest?

IB DP Mathematics AI SL · Voronoi diagrams

Which station is closest?

Three rescue stations serve a region. Predict which station should respond to the incident, then reveal the regions to check your prediction. Move the incident to see where the answer changes.

Tap the map or use the sliders. Coordinates are in kilometres.

Explore the mathematics

I would ask you to find the midpoint of A(3, 2) and B(9, 2), then explain why every point on the line x = 6 is equally far from A and B. Turn on the regions and follow that boundary upwards. Where does it stop, and why?

Check your reasoning

I would compare the distance to C(8, 6). At (6, 3.375), all three distances are equal. Above that point, C is closer than either A or B, so the A–B boundary ends there. In this model, we use straight-line distance. How might roads or hills change the fastest response?

IGCSE · Circle theoremsCan you change the angle?

IGCSE · Circle theorems

Can you change the angle?

Drag P around the circle. Predict what happens to angle APB when P crosses chord AB to the other arc.

Explore the mathematics

I would ask you to move P several times along the longer arc. What stays the same? The angle is 45° each time because angles subtended by the same chord in the same segment are equal. Now try the shorter arc. When you see 135°, explain how the two angles are related.

Try a practice question

In a new diagram, A, B, P and Q lie on a circle. P and Q are on opposite sides of chord AB. If angle APB is 68°, what is angle AQB?

Check your answer

112°. I would ask you to trace the quadrilateral APBQ and tell me which angles are opposite. In a cyclic quadrilateral, opposite angles add to 180°, so I would subtract 68° from 180°.

Mathematics enrichment · Make 21Make 21

Mathematics enrichment · Game strategy

Make 21

You go first. Take turns adding 1, 2 or 3. The first player to reach exactly 21 wins.

YouTotal 0Computer

    Your turn. Choose how much to add.

    Explore the strategy

    I would start by asking what happens if you leave your opponent on 17. Try all three moves they could make. How would you reply each time to reach 21? Then work backwards and see if you can find an earlier total that gives you the same advantage.

    I would then ask how your strategy changes if we vary one rule at a time. What if the target were 25? What if we started at 5 instead of 0, or allowed each player to add 1, 2, 3 or 4?
